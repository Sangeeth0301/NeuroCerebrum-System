Fair evaluation shrinks every seizure-forecasting headline

47 conference and journal papers across six areas: what each did, how, what it achieved, and what is left for our project to do first.

36 journal · 9 conference · 2 preprintDataset focus: TUH EEG Seizure Corpus (TUSZ)Local files: research/papers/

Main finding: when tests use new, unseen patients and count whole seizures, almost every impressive number in this field becomes much smaller. On TUSZ, nobody has yet reported honest, alarm-level seizure prediction or causal onset latency.

The literature does not lack models. It lacks fair, comparable measurements of time. A project that reports honest numbers together with lead time, onset latency and per-type delay would be a real contribution even without beating an accuracy record.

P · 8 papers

Prediction on scalp EEG: honest numbers fall from 97% to AUC 0.7

Kuhlmann 2018 (Kaggle contest on implant EEG) and Truong 2018 (STFT-CNN, one model per patient) set the rules still used: time-separated test data, whole-seizure scoring with SPH/SOP windows, and a random-predictor comparison. Truong reached 81.2% sensitivity at 0.16 false alarms/h on 13 CHB-MIT patients.

Dissanayake 2022, Koutsouvelis 2024 and Meng 2025 report very high numbers under splits that let the test patient into training. Koutsouvelis' famous 76.8 min is when the smoothed output converges, not a real alarm, and its false-alarm rate is 33.6/h per segment.

Only Jemal 2024 and CG-MambaNet 2026 (preprint) test truly unseen patients. The same CNN drops from 97.4% pooled to AUC 0.69 leave-one-patient-out. MLSPred-Bench 2025 is the only TUSZ prediction benchmark, with validation segment AUC only, and it swaps the SPH and SOP names.

What we learn

Changing the split from pooled to leave-one-patient-out costs about 35 accuracy points. Only ~4% of prediction papers validate cross-patient (abs). There is no agreed definition of "warning time". TUSZ sessions are short, so fewer seizures qualify as the horizon grows (1,282 seizures at a 3-min horizon, fewer at 35 min).

D · 7 papers

Detection and latency: accuracy is measured, "how early" is not

Shah 2018 introduced TUSZ. Saab 2020 showed short clips hurt (F1 0.49 at 12 s vs 0.76 at 60 s). Lee 2022 is the only patient-independent latency study on TUSZ: a 4-s window streamed every second through 15 models, with mean onset latency 7.76–15.83 s. It also shows scoring decides everything: the same model gets 0.75 sensitivity at 47 FA/24h (OVLP) but 0.33 at 1.03 FA/24h (TAES).

Xu 2023 uses soft labels on onset-crossing windows plus evidence accumulation: 2.3 s latency, but one model per patient. SzCORE is the scoring standard, with −30/+60 s tolerance and no latency metric. SeizureTransformer (preprint) holds the best TUSZ event F1, 0.671. Zabihi 2026 is an honest CatBoost benchmark: sensitivity 0.75 at 0.68 FA/h; its "0 s latency" is a dilation artifact.

What we learn

SzCORE scores a 25-s-late alarm the same as a 1-s alarm. The only fair TUSZ latency (~8–16 s) is on an old version and a small test set. 2–5 s results are patient-specific. Offline tricks (long windows, future context, dilation) fake zero latency. Short focal seizures are the hardest, and no paper reports latency per seizure type.

C · 8 papers

Seizure type and spread: fair type scores near 0.65–0.75, spread shown only in pictures

Tang 2022 (ICLR) is the reference: a graph neural network over 19 electrodes with self-supervised pretraining, patient-wise on TUSZ, 4 merged classes (focal, generalized, absence, tonic). wF1 0.749 (4-class), 0.650 (7-class). A neurologist found 27 of 32 "generalized" errors were really focal, so TUSZ labels are noisy. SeizureNet proves the leakage effect: wF1 0.95–0.98 seizure-wise vs 0.62 patient-wise.

For spread: SZTrack tracks per-channel seizure activity over time (hemisphere + lobe correct in 21/34 patients). DeepSIF trains on neural-mass simulations and localizes onset within 7–11 mm, but needs 76-channel EEG. Neural fragility predicts surgical outcome from iEEG with AUC 0.88.

What we learn

Leakage inflates type scores by ~0.33 wF1. Classes with two patients cannot be evaluated fairly, so the 4-class merge is the fair standard. No paper combines type and spread, and none measures spread accuracy with numbers. DeepSIF has not been tested on 19-channel clinical EEG like TUSZ.

N · 9 papers

Neuroscience models: real warning signals live mostly in slow cycles

The strongest forecasting evidence comes from months of implant recordings. Baud 2018: spike rates follow 20–30 day cycles and seizures cluster on the rising phase. Proix 2021: cycle-based daily forecasts, AUC 0.74 / 0.70 in 175 patients. Maturana 2020: critical slowing down gives 77% sensitivity with 91% of time at low risk. Chang 2018 is the counterpoint: autocorrelation rose before seizures in only 4/12 patients and fell in 4/12.

For the E/I digital twin: Wendling 2002 and Jirsa 2014 give the models. Karoly 2018 tracked Jansen–Rit parameters over 3,010 seizures, but only during seizures. Duma 2025, the only scalp study, found the aperiodic exponent rising ~13 min before seizures, a shift toward inhibition, not excitation.

What we learn

Honest biomarker forecasting reaches AUC ~0.70–0.80, mostly from day-to-week cycles that TUSZ is too short to capture. On TUSZ we can only test minutes-scale markers. Our "excitation rises" hypothesis is not what the one scalp study found, so both directions must be allowed. Sleep and artifacts also move these markers and must be controlled.

F · 7 papers

Foundation models: small gains for seizures, and a hidden TUSZ overlap

BENDR → BIOT → LaBraM → EEGPT → CBraMod share one benchmark (TUAB, TUEV, CHB-MIT with 2 test patients). None reports TUSZ seizure results. LaBraM put 1,138 hours of TUSZ into pretraining; BENDR and CBraMod used the whole TUH archive. EEGPT used no TUH data, so it is a leakage-free control. Independent benchmarks (EEG-Bench, EEG-FM-Compass) find specialist models remain competitive.

B · 8 papers

Body reaction: the heart often reacts seconds before scalp EEG, but not reliably

Heart rate rises in ~87% of focal seizures, on average 8–14 s before scalp-EEG onset, but the spread (±15–22 s) is as large as the mean, and it depends on side (right temporal before, left after). Wearable ECG and wrist devices detect seizures 28–38 s after onset, so they are detectors, not predictors. Heart-rate cycles from a smartwatch can forecast risk hours ahead. No paper uses the ECG channel inside TUSZ.

Watch out

Common pitfalls that inflate or confuse results

Patient leakage

Pooled or segment-shuffled splits add ~35 accuracy points in prediction and ~0.33 wF1 in type classification. "Subject-independent" and "patient-independent" can still include the test patient. Leave-one-seizure-out trains on future seizures. Foundation models pretrained on TUH have likely seen TUSZ eval EEG.

Scoring differences

Segment vs event false alarms differ by more than 300× (112.4/h vs 0.32/h). OVLP vs TAES changes sensitivity from 0.75 to 0.33. SzCORE's tolerance hides lateness. Dilation fakes "0 s" latency. MLSPred-Bench swaps SPH and SOP names.

Dataset versions and label noise

TUSZ v1.2.0, v1.4, v1.5.2 and v2.0.3 differ. 27 of 32 checked "generalized" errors were really focal. Preictal windows can include the previous seizure's recovery if no buffer is set.

Abstract-only, preprint and inconsistent numbers

Several numbers conflict between versions (challenge F1 43% vs 32%; MLSPred seizure counts; CG-MambaNet tables). Always cite the PDF tables.

Small test sets

Foundation-model CHB-MIT benchmarks use 2 test patients. Per-patient AUCs range 0.29–0.96 in Jemal. Report confidence intervals across patients.

Honest limits. TUSZ cannot test the multi-day cycles that drive most validated forecasting skill, so "earlier" on TUSZ means minutes, not days. There is no human evidence yet that earlier alarms improve outcomes; it is an engineering goal.

Entry checklist for the approach

Implications for choosing our approach

  1. Fix the protocol before choosing models: official TUSZ v2.0.x split, thresholds frozen on dev, SzCORE event metrics, conventional SPH/SOP naming, interictal and postictal buffers, and a chance or surrogate test.
  2. Define latency and warning time causally, and report distributions (median/IQR, % detected within 5 s and 10 s), latency vs false alarms per day, and results per seizure type.
  3. Include cheap strong baselines: RF/CatBoost on features and a Truong-style STFT-CNN, next to any deep model.
  4. If a foundation model is used, compare with a random-initialized copy and a leakage-free control (EEGPT), and check TUH patient overlap.
  5. Treat alarm post-processing (k-of-n, persistence, refractory period, accumulation) as a tuned, reported part of the system.
  6. For type and spread, use patient-wise 4-class labels and design a numerical spread metric from TUSZ channel annotations.
  7. For E/I and body modules, allow both E/I directions, control for sleep and artifacts, and first check ECG availability in TUSZ.
  8. Add at least one external dataset (Siena or CHB-MIT) because of the large cross-site gap.
