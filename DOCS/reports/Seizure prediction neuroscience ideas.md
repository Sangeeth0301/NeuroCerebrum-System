# Predict seizures by reading hidden brain dynamics

**Main answer: you cannot see single neurons or synapses in scalp EEG. You can still build a novel and useful seizure-*prediction* project on TUSZ. The idea is to fit a small computational brain model to the EEG and read "effective" excitation and inhibition values from it. You then test honestly whether these values, together with a few simple physics-based warning signs, rise before seizures.** The neuroscience shows two different timescales. The final trigger happens in seconds and at sub-millimetre scale: interneurons fire very fast, they stop working (depolarization block), potassium builds up outside the cells, and pyramidal cells are released from inhibition. None of this is visible on the scalp. The brain's *susceptibility* to a seizure changes more slowly, over minutes to days, and it leaves population-level traces that scalp EEG *can* measure. These traces include rising variance and autocorrelation ("critical slowing down"), a change in the slope of the EEG spectrum, and changes in spike rate. Neural mass models such as Wendling/Jansen-Rit can be inverted with simulation-based inference (SBI) to estimate excitatory and inhibitory synaptic gains from each EEG window. No one appears to have done this on TUSZ, and the one old study that predicted seizures from fitted-model features used an evaluation that is weaker than today's standards. Be realistic about performance. Under honest, patient-independent testing, scalp prediction reaches about **AUC 0.70–0.82, 60–75% seizure sensitivity, 0.3–0.6 false alarms per hour, and roughly 20 minutes of warning**. The 90–99% figures common in papers are mostly artefacts of data leakage. TUSZ also has a special weakness: its recordings are short, so it can support only short-horizon prediction (minutes), not forecasts over days. **Our recommended project is "NeuroMech-Warn".** It is a mechanism-based early-warning system that combines SBI-estimated E/I parameters with critical-slowing and spectral-slope features. It runs a calibrated risk alarm, is tested on TUSZ and on the longer CHB-MIT/Siena recordings, and must beat a seizure-time surrogate test. It is novel, it fits one consumer GPU in 4–6 months, and its main risks can be managed.

## Seizures start in seconds inside cells but build up for hours in networks

**Key terms used in this report**

| Term | Simple meaning |
|---|---|
| **Preictal** | The period *before* a seizure in which the brain is already changing |
| **Pro-ictal** | A period of raised seizure *risk* that does not always end in a seizure |
| **E/I balance** | The balance between excitation (neurons pushing each other to fire) and inhibition (neurons calming each other) |
| **Interneuron (PV, SST)** | Inhibitory neurons. PV cells are fast; SST cells act on dendrites |
| **Depolarization block** | A neuron is so over-excited that it cannot fire any more, so it stops inhibiting others |
| **Critical slowing down (CSD)** | Near a tipping point, a system recovers slowly from small pushes. Variance and autocorrelation rise |
| **Aperiodic (1/f) exponent** | The slope of the EEG power spectrum without the rhythmic peaks. A steeper slope is often read as more inhibition |
| **SPH / SOP** | Seizure prediction horizon (the gap between alarm and the start of the risk window) and seizure occurrence period (the window in which the seizure must happen) |
| **Neural mass model (NMM)** | Equations that describe the *average* activity of groups of neurons, not single cells |
| **SBI** | Simulation-based inference: you simulate the model many times, then train a neural network to guess the model's parameters from data |

At the level of cells, the last seconds before a focal seizure follow an **interneuron-driven sequence**. In a 4-AP brain-slice model, interneurons fired at high frequency and then went into depolarization block. This happened at the moment when extracellular potassium was rising fastest. Pyramidal cells started firing **1.1 ± 0.3 s later**, in 100% of recordings ([PMC12295800](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12295800/)). In mice, PV interneurons showed a sharp late burst of firing just before onset, while SST cells were raised for longer and slowly stopped responding to input. Synaptic inhibition itself stayed intact, so the problem looks like *poorly timed* inhibition rather than missing inhibition ([eLife 40750](https://elifesciences.org/articles/40750)). Chloride transport (KCC2) adds a paradox. KCC2 restores inhibition, but it also pushes potassium out of cells, so blocking it has been reported to be both pro- and anti-seizure ([Nat Commun 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-08933-4)). Human microelectrode arrays show that seizures do not start as "all neurons firing together". At initiation, single-neuron firing is **heterogeneous, not hypersynchronous** ([Truccolo 2011, Nat Neurosci](https://www.nature.com/articles/nn.2782)). A sharp border separates the recruited "ictal core" from a "penumbra" that is held back by an inhibitory veto, and macroscale EEG cannot tell these two regions apart ([Schevon 2012, Nat Commun](https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms2056)).

At the level of networks, the story is slower and more useful for prediction. Chang et al. showed in animals that the move into seizure is **a gradual loss of resilience governed by critical slowing**. Whether an interictal spike (a burst of synchronous synaptic input) triggers a seizure depends on this resilience state ([Nat Neurosci 2018](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-018-0278-y)). In humans, Maturana et al. analysed 14 NeuroVista implant patients over 7,246 recording days. Variance and autocorrelation followed daily and roughly 10-day cycles, and seizures fell on the rising phase. In **9 of 14 patients** these features rose gradually over tens of minutes to hours before lead seizures. A pseudo-prospective forecast reached **77 ± 8% sensitivity with only 8 ± 6% time in warning**, against about 9% for a random predictor ([Nat Commun 2020](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-020-15908-3)). Two caveats matter. First, CSD is expected only when the brain is pushed *slowly* toward a tipping point, not when a random push causes the seizure. The Saggio "dynamotype" taxonomy found 16 different onset/offset routes in more than 2,000 seizures ([eLife 2020](https://elifesciences.org/articles/55632)). Second, a 2025 paper argues that CSD marks a **pro-ictal** state (raised risk) rather than an imminent seizure ([Clin Neurophysiol 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1388245725005942)).

The most surprising finding concerns E/I balance. A naive story says "excitation rises until the seizure starts". The best recent data show the opposite before onset. On 128-channel scalp EEG in 20 patients, the aperiodic exponent **rose steadily over the 13 minutes before seizures**, which suggests a global shift *toward inhibition*. The authors read this as a compensatory inhibition that finally fails ([Duma 2025, BMC Med](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12581463/)). Intracranial RNS data show a similar rise over the 12 hours before seizures, and stimulation *lowered* the exponent ([Brain Stimulation 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1935861X25000798)). This fits the cell data, where inhibitory cells are *hyperactive* before onset. Interictal spike rate behaves in a similar counter-intuitive way. It follows multiday cycles and seizures cluster on its rising phase ([Baud 2018, Nat Commun](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-017-02577-y)), yet it can *fall* just before seizures ([Karoly 2016, Brain](https://academic.oup.com/brain/article-abstract/139/4/1066/2464379)). Older claims that synchrony drops before seizures ([Mormann 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12694925/)) failed later statistical controls ([Kuhlmann 2018, Nat Rev Neurol](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41582-018-0055-2)). **So the scientifically interesting question is not "does excitation go up?" but "does a measurable inhibitory compensation build up and then fail, and can a model see it?"**

## Scalp EEG sees crowds of neurons, never single synapses

Here your expectations must be adjusted. A scalp-visible spike usually needs **about 10–20 cm² of gyral cortex firing synchronously** ([Tao 2005, Epilepsia](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15857432/)). That patch holds hundreds of millions of neurons. Deep sources such as the hippocampus are often invisible on the scalp ([Epilepsy Res 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0920121122000651)). Weaker, less synchronous generators can still change spectral features, so the 10 cm² rule applies to *visible spikes*, not to every feature ([NeuroImage 2016](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1053811916301884)). Scalp EEG also lacks DC shifts and, in TUSZ, very high frequencies. As a result, the onset "bifurcation type" could be identified in only **about 49.5% of 1,177 scalp seizures** ([Guendelman 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11747977/)). TUSZ is clinical EEG at about 250 Hz on a 10–20 montage ([Shah 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neuroinformatics/articles/10.3389/fninf.2018.00083/full)), so high-frequency oscillations (HFOs) above about 100 Hz cannot be measured in it at all.

| Mechanism | What scalp EEG can show | Timing before seizure | Where it has been measured |
|---|---|---|---|
| Interneuron burst, depolarization block, K⁺ build-up | Nothing directly | Seconds | Slices, animals, microelectrodes |
| Ictal core vs penumbra, single-neuron recruitment | Nothing | Seconds | Microelectrodes only |
| Loss of resilience (critical slowing) | Variance, lag-1 autocorrelation, slower recovery after spikes | Tens of minutes to hours; daily cycles | Implants (strong); scalp not yet rigorously tested |
| E/I set-point shift | Aperiodic exponent; model-fitted synaptic gains | About 13 min (scalp), about 12 h (RNS) | High-density scalp EEG, implants |
| Interictal spikes (synaptic "pushes") | Spike rate | Cycles over days; may fall just before | Scalp and implants |
| Network synchrony | wPLI or imaginary coherence (to reduce volume-conduction errors) | Minutes to hours | Implants; contested on scalp |
| Complexity | Spectral entropy, Hjorth mobility | 83 ± 60 min in one small study | Scalp, weak evidence |

*Sources for the table: [Tao 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15857432/), [Maturana 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7195436/), [Duma 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12581463/), [Baud 2018](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-017-02577-y), [Segal 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12082717/), [volume conduction, arXiv 1409.4251](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1409.4251).*

What does this mean for your project? Think of scalp EEG as a microphone outside a football stadium. You cannot hear any single fan, but you can hear the crowd get louder, change rhythm, and become slower to calm down after each goal. The "crowd" signals you can realistically use on TUSZ are variance and autocorrelation per channel, recovery time after each spontaneous interictal spike (a scalp version of Chang's "push and watch the recovery" test), the aperiodic exponent, band powers, spike rate and entropy. Each one has confounds. The aperiodic exponent changes with sleep and wakefulness ([PMC11315276](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11315276/)) and with antiseizure medication ([bioRxiv 2025](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.11.02.686141.full.pdf)). Spike waveforms themselves can distort the slope fit and look like an E/I shift ([PubMed 41213802](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41213802/)). A careful project must control for these confounds, not ignore them.

Single-neuron recordings do not rescue the "see the neurons" dream either. In neocortex, some neurons, often *outside* the onset zone, change their firing **minutes** before a seizure, and interneurons more often than principal cells ([Truccolo 2011](https://www.nature.com/articles/nn.2782)). Multi-unit activity recorded 2–3 cm away from the onset zone separated preictal from interictal periods with **AUC up to about 0.9**. That study had only 2–3 seizures per patient and tuned its settings on test data ([Proix 2019, PLOS One](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6645464/)). In the hippocampus, 156 units from 19 patients showed **no systematic firing change in the 5 minutes before onset**, and inhibition was preserved there ([Agopyan-Miu 2023, Brain](https://academic.oup.com/brain/article/146/12/5209/7236754)). A criticality analysis of single units in 20 patients found **no drift toward instability** before seizures ([Hagemann, arXiv 2004.10642](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.10642)). So even with electrodes inside the brain, the useful preictal signal is a *population* signal, not the behaviour of one neuron.

## Brain models let you estimate synaptic gains indirectly, with limits

Computational models are the honest way to "see" synapses from EEG. They do not show real synapses. They estimate *effective* synaptic parameters that best explain the signal. The Jansen-Rit model reduces a cortical column to three populations. Wendling's extension adds a second inhibitory group, so there are slow dendritic and fast somatic inhibitory interneurons. It explains the fast gamma activity at seizure onset as a failure of dendritic inhibition ([Wendling 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12028360/)). Its parameters have physiological names: **A ≈ 3.25 mV (excitatory gain), B ≈ 22 mV (slow inhibitory gain), G ≈ 20 mV (fast inhibitory gain)**, plus time constants ([U Twente summary](https://www.utwente.nl/en/eemcs/magnus/teaching/Thesis/hebbink-jurgen-activity-types-in-a-neural-mass-model-fp-report-final.pdf)). A ratio such as A/B or A/G then acts as a *model-based E/I index*. The Epileptor is different. It is a phenomenological model with an excitability parameter (x0) and a slow "permittivity" variable that pushes the system into and out of seizures ([Jirsa 2014, Brain](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4107736/)). It is excellent for simulating onset types and for building whole-brain "virtual patients", but x0 means "how epileptogenic this region is", not "synaptic strength".

There are four ways to fit these models to EEG. **Kalman filters** track parameters sample by sample. Karoly et al. fitted Jansen-Rit to NeuroVista iEEG covering 3,010 seizures in 12 patients. They found patient-specific, repeating "seizure pathways" with three onset types (pyramidal input connectivity going down, going up, or down-then-up), and their code is public ([PLoS CB 2018](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pcbi.1006403); [GitHub](https://github.com/pkaroly/Data-Driven-Estimation)). An unscented Kalman filter (UKF) has estimated Jansen-Rit parameters from *scalp-level* signals through a head model, although only in simulation ([arXiv 1708.05282](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05282)). **Dynamic causal modelling (DCM)** has described seizure onset in rats as slow changes in synaptic excitability ([Papadopoulou 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27639356)), but it is slow to run on every window. **SBI** trains a neural network once on simulations and then gives a posterior (a distribution of likely parameter values) for any window in milliseconds. Hashemi et al. used it for Epileptor networks on SEEG ([Neural Networks 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37060871/)), and the `sbi` toolkit makes it practical ([arXiv 2411.17337](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.17337)). **Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs)** that estimate E/I from ECoG exist only as early conference work ([ACM ICBRA](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3700666.3700701)).

The cost is small enough for a student. In the VBI toolkit benchmarks, training an SBI network on **50,000 Jansen-Rit simulations took about 45 minutes**, and 10,000 Epileptor simulations took under 1 minute to generate and about 13 minutes to train ([VBI, eLife 2025](https://elifesciences.org/articles/106194)). After training, thousands of TUSZ windows can be processed on a laptop. The closest predecessor to a model-based predictor is Aarabi & He. They fitted 12 NMM parameters and reported **79% sensitivity at 0.16 false predictions per hour on CHB-MIT scalp EEG** ([Clin Neurophysiol 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24374087/)). That was in 2014, before surrogate testing and pseudo-prospective evaluation became standard. A modern, honest re-test is therefore a real gap. DeepSIF shows that deep networks trained on Jansen-Rit simulations can work on real scalp data: it located seizure sources in 33 patients within **10.9 ± 10.1 mm** of the onset zone ([Adv Sci 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11653641/)).

There is one big caveat, and you must state it in your thesis. **Many parameter combinations produce almost the same EEG spectrum.** This is called poor identifiability, and it is why a 2024 paper recommends sensitivity analysis *before* claiming that any synaptic gain is being tracked ([Phys Rev E 2024](https://journals.aps.org/pre/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevE.110.044208)). Scalp volume conduction also mixes sources, so a model fitted per channel gives "lumped" parameters, not the synapses of one cortical column. Finally, the whole-brain Virtual Epileptic Patient answers *where* seizures start, not *when*. The EPINOV trial (more than 300 patients, active phase closed in November 2023) had no published primary results in the sources we found ([INS-AMU](https://ins-amu.fr/epinov)), and we found no study that uses a digital twin to forecast seizure timing. Using these models to answer "when?" is therefore open territory.

## Honest scalp prediction gives about 20 minutes of warning at AUC 0.7–0.8

Most published scalp-prediction numbers cannot be trusted. A 2026 systematic review of 17 scalp-EEG prediction studies found that **76.5% used CHB-MIT, none used TUSZ, and none had a low risk of bias**. The main problem was leakage from overlapping windows or non-independent train/test data ([Alasade 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13318060/)). When evaluation is done properly, with leave-one-patient-out testing over 5 seeds, the numbers become modest. CG-MambaNet reached **AUC 0.815, 74.3% sensitivity, 0.32 false alarms per hour and 23.4 minutes mean lead time on CHB-MIT**, and AUC 0.710 with 63.2% sensitivity on Siena. Its authors note that counting false alarms per window instead of per event changes the rate about **351-fold** (112.4/h vs 0.32/h) ([arXiv 2606.08226](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.08226)). The Kaggle/Melbourne contest shows the same drop on implant data: the winner fell from AUC 0.85 to 0.81 on private data and to **0.75 on truly held-out continuous data** ([Kuhlmann 2018, Brain](https://academic.oup.com/brain/article/141/9/2619/5066003)).

TUSZ has exactly one prediction benchmark. MLSPred-Bench converts TUSZ into 12 patient-independent tasks (208 training, 45 validation and 34 test subjects; 5-s windows). Its best raw-EEG ResNet reached **70.3% sensitivity but only 59.6% specificity**, reported per segment, with no alarm-level results, and the authors themselves warn of leakage at longer horizons ([MLSPred-Bench, PMC12423417](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12423417/)). The benchmark also *swaps* the usual meanings of SPH and SOP, so you must state which convention you use. More important, TUSZ recordings are mostly short clinical sessions. Many seizures lack long seizure-free periods before them, and filtering for them removes much of the data. TUSZ therefore suits **short-horizon prediction (warning minutes ahead) and mechanism studies**, not forecasts over days. The total recording hours reported for TUSZ also differ between papers (about 1,476 h vs about 10,000 h), so check the README of the version you download ([MLSPred-Bench](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12423417/)).

**Realistic warning times depend on the data you have.** With chronic implants, CSD and spike-rate cycles give forecasts from hours to days. RNS data allowed forecasts up to **3 days ahead** in 157 validation patients ([Proix 2021, Lancet Neurol](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/laneur/article/PIIS1474-4422(20)30396-3/abstract)). Wrist wearables reached **AUC 0.78 with 6.3 h/day in warning**, above chance in 10 of 11 participants ([Nasseri 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12344589/)). On scalp EEG, honest cross-patient work gives about **20 minutes**. One small study reported preictal changes **83 ± 60 minutes** ahead in 89.5% of seizures, but it had only 21 patients and no prospective test ([Segal 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12082717/)). **Treat any cross-patient scalp result above about 90% sensitivity with fewer than 0.1 false alarms per hour as probable leakage until proven otherwise.** No study we found compares how predictable different seizure types are in humans, although spike-wave discharges in rat models of absence epilepsy were predictable above chance ([eNeuro 2021](https://www.eneuro.org/content/9/1/ENEURO.0160-21.2021)). TUSZ's eight type labels make this question testable.

## Implants and BCIs show where prediction becomes prevention

Neuralink-style devices are the natural future home for this kind of work, but they are not epilepsy products today. We found no primary evidence of any Neuralink epilepsy trial, and media reports mention seizures only as a possible future use ([CNBC 2022](https://www.cnbc.com/2022/12/01/elon-musks-neuralink-makes-big-claims-but-experts-are-skeptical-.html)). Precision's Layer 7 thin-film array is FDA-cleared for up to 30 days and includes epilepsy-surgery mapping among its uses, and it recorded **4,096 electrodes at once** in a human ([FDA K242618](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf24/K242618.pdf)). Paradromics' first human recording happened during epilepsy surgery, but the device's goal is communication ([Michigan Medicine](https://www.michiganmedicine.org/news-release/university-michigan-implants-first-human-paradromics-wireless-brain-computer-interface-designed)). Neuralink's 2019 paper gives **5.2 µW per analog channel and 550–750 mW per system** ([JMIR 2019](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6914248/)), so any seizure model running on an implant must be tiny. Two findings shape how such a device should work. Multi-unit activity *alone* (spike counts without sorting spikes into single neurons) was predictive ([Proix 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6645464/)), and it is cheap to compute on a chip. Neurons far from the onset zone also carried preictal information, so an array does not need to sit exactly on the focus. This is useful because BCI arrays sit in motor and speech cortex, not in the temporal lobe. **This is the link to your project:** a neural mass model is also a compact, physics-based "feature extractor". The same SBI pipeline you build for scalp EEG could run on ECoG or on multi-unit summaries from an implant.

Prediction matters most when it triggers *therapy*, not only a warning. The strongest recent "prevention" result came from kindled rats. Real-time detection of hippocampal interictal spikes triggered prefrontal stimulation, and this **prevented progression to bilateral convulsive seizures** (p = 2.4×10⁻³) and preserved memory ([Ferrero 2025, Nat Neurosci](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12321579/)). On-demand optogenetic activation of PV interneurons stopped spontaneous seizures in mice ([Krook-Magnuson 2013](https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms2376)). A simulation study of 24-hour EEG from about 100 patients found that timing a short-half-life drug about **6 hours before peak risk** gave a 62.4% reduction in epileptiform discharges vs 45.3% at the worst timing, with the same total dose ([Ahern 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12891084/)). Together these results suggest three warning tiers: **seconds** for stimulation, **minutes to about an hour** for a rescue drug or for getting to a safe place, and **hours to days** for adjusting medication. A TUSZ project fits the middle tier. We found no human trial of therapy triggered by *prediction* rather than by seizure onset.

Patient needs also shape how the output should look. In a survey of 652 people, **65% would keep using a device after a false alarm, but only 52% would continue if a seizure happened during a "low-risk" forecast**, and 60% wanted both high- and low-risk forecasts ([Frontiers Neurol 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurology/articles/10.3389/fneur.2021.717428/full)). So an honest system should output a *calibrated risk*, and it should report how often "low risk" was wrong. The NeuroVista implant, the only prospective prediction device, reached 65–100% sensitivity in the patients where it worked ([Cook 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23642342/)), but it failed commercially partly because regulators did not know how to judge a "warning-only" device ([Karoly, Epilepsia](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/epi.70394)).

### Idea 1: read model-based excitation and inhibition before each seizure

**Hypothesis.** Wendling-model parameters estimated by amortized SBI from each scalp-EEG window (A, B, G, time constants, and A/B, A/G ratios) change in a consistent direction in the 5–30 minutes before seizures. Following Duma 2025, the expected direction is *rising* inhibitory gain. These parameters add predictive information beyond spectral features alone, and the size of the effect differs between focal and generalized seizure types. **Method.** Simulate 50,000–100,000 Wendling runs with wide priors, compute power spectra, and train a neural posterior estimator (NPE) with `sbi`. First check which parameters are identifiable: fit simulated data with known values and measure how well each one is recovered, as the 2024 Phys Rev E paper recommends ([Phys Rev E](https://journals.aps.org/pre/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevE.110.044208)). Apply the network to 10–30 s windows on each bipolar channel and keep the posterior means *and* uncertainties. Feed these trajectories into a small model (logistic regression, gradient boosting, or a small GRU) that outputs a risk score. **Datasets.** TUSZ for mechanism analysis and short-horizon prediction; CHB-MIT and Siena for longer horizons and cross-dataset tests; SWEC-ETHZ iEEG optionally, to check whether the same parameters move on implant data. **Metrics.** Seizure sensitivity, event-level false alarms per hour, time in warning, AUC, Brier score, improvement over chance, and number of patients above chance. **Baselines.** Spectral power + aperiodic features only; the Kaggle-winner feature set (spectral power, Hjorth, entropy, fractal dimension) ([Brain 2018](https://academic.oup.com/brain/article/141/9/2619/5066003)); a raw-EEG CNN/ResNet as in MLSPred-Bench; Aarabi & He's reported 79% at 0.16/h as a historical reference ([PubMed 24374087](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24374087/)). **Why novel:** no published work fits neural mass models to TUSZ, and no 2018–2026 study reports honest forecasting metrics from fitted-model parameters.

### Idea 2: measure the brain's loss of resilience from the scalp

**Hypothesis.** Lag-1 autocorrelation and variance rise, and recovery after spontaneous interictal spikes slows down, in the 10–60 minutes before scalp seizures. The effect appears mainly for seizures whose onset looks like a slow approach to a tipping point. **Method.** Compute sliding-window variance and autocorrelation (following the open Maturana code ([GitHub](https://github.com/tempo-beme/Critical_Slowing_Epilepsy))), detect spikes automatically, and measure how fast the signal returns to baseline after each spike. As an extension, label seizure onsets with a simplified Saggio dynamotype and test whether CSD appears only for certain onset types ([Guendelman 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11747977/)). **Datasets.** CHB-MIT and Siena (long recordings) first, then TUSZ sessions with enough preictal time. **Metrics and baselines.** The same alarm metrics as Idea 1, compared against seizure-time surrogates and a spike-rate-only predictor. Maturana's iEEG result (77% sensitivity at 8% time in warning) is the upper reference ([Nat Commun 2020](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-020-15908-3)). **Why useful:** no rigorous scalp CSD study with surrogate testing was found, and the features are cheap enough for any device.

### Idea 3: build the first honest TUSZ prediction benchmark

**Hypothesis.** When MLSPred-Bench-style models are scored per alarm with surrogate tests, most configurations will not beat chance, and predictability will differ by seizure type. **Method.** Re-split TUSZ by patient, use the standard SPH/SOP definitions, merge alarms into events, calibrate risk, and report results per seizure type (FNSZ, GNSZ, CPSZ, ABSZ, TCSZ) with patient-level bootstrap confidence intervals. **Baselines.** The MLSPred-Bench ResNet and random forest ([PMC12423417](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12423417/)). **Value:** this fills a gap the systematic review found directly (zero TUSZ studies), and it becomes the evaluation backbone for Ideas 1 and 2.

### Idea 4: test whether the "rise in inhibition" survives confounds

**Hypothesis.** The preictal increase in the aperiodic exponent (Duma 2025) appears on standard 19-channel clinical EEG, but part of it is explained by sleep state, time of day and spike contamination. **Method.** Run time-resolved specparam/SPRiNT, remove spike segments, add automatic sleep staging and time of day as covariates, and compare preictal segments with matched interictal segments. **Risk:** this is a correlation study with a lower ceiling, so it works best as one chapter of Idea 1.

### Idea 5: compare single-neuron-scale and field features before seizures

**Hypothesis.** In the EBRAINS Utah-array data, multi-unit activity and spike-band power change before seizures earlier than low-frequency LFP does. **Method.** Compute threshold-crossing rates and band powers on the 10-minute preictal windows and compare them with interictal baselines. **Limits:** only 12 seizures in 5 patients ([PMC12065801](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12065801/)), so the results can only be descriptive. This idea is the direct bridge to Neuralink-style devices, but it should be treated as an optional side study.

## Build NeuroMech-Warn, and prove it beats chance

**Recommended pick: Idea 1, with Idea 2's features and Idea 3's evaluation built in.** This combination answers your real question ("what is happening to excitation and inhibition before a seizure?") in the only physically possible way. It creates something new (the first SBI-based E/I tracking on TUSZ), and it still produces a useful result if prediction turns out weak. A clean negative result, such as "model-based E/I features do not beat chance cross-patient on TUSZ", would also be publishable, because the field lacks honest scalp results. The compute fits one consumer GPU, since SBI training takes about an hour ([VBI](https://elifesciences.org/articles/106194)), and everything after that runs on a CPU.

**Mandatory honest evaluation protocol.** These rules follow the Kuhlmann 2018 guidelines ([Nat Rev Neurol](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41582-018-0055-2)), the 2026 systematic review ([Alasade 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13318060/)), surrogate-tested forecasting ([Nasseri 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12344589/)) and the leakage analysis in [arXiv 2606.08226](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.08226).

| Rule | What to do | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Patient-disjoint splits | No patient appears in both training and test; never shuffle windows across splits | Random splits cause most inflated results |
| Declare SPH, SOP, preictal and interictal definitions | Use the Winterhalder meaning of SPH/SOP; state interictal buffers and the "lead seizure" rule | MLSPred-Bench uses swapped definitions |
| Alarm-level metrics first | Seizure sensitivity, event-level false alarms per hour, % time in warning, AUC, Brier score; window-level numbers only as secondary | Window-level false-alarm rates can be about 351× higher |
| Beat chance | Seizure-time surrogates (≥200 shuffles) or an analytic random predictor; report improvement over chance and the number of patients above chance | Proves the signal is real |
| Tune on validation only | Thresholds, hyperparameters and calibration fixed before touching test data; report test once | Prevents hidden overfitting |
| Report by seizure type, with confidence intervals | Patient-level bootstrap; flag small classes (ABSZ, MYSZ, TNSZ) | TUSZ type counts are unbalanced |
| Cross-dataset test | Train on one dataset, test on another without retraining | New hospitals cut performance sharply |
| Control confounds | Time of day, sleep/wake, spike contamination, recording-session cues | The aperiodic exponent and CSD depend on state |
| State the limits | TUSZ gives minutes, not days; scalp E/I parameters are lumped model estimates, not real synapses | Keeps claims honest |

## Conclusion

The main change in understanding is this: seizure prediction is less about catching a sudden burst of excitation and more about tracking a slow loss of resilience in which inhibition works harder and then fails. Scalp EEG cannot show you the neurons doing this. A fitted brain model, however, can turn EEG into a small set of interpretable numbers that summarize it, and those numbers can be tested with the same strict statistics used for any predictor. This moves your contribution away from "yet another deep network with 99% accuracy" and toward a measurement that neuroscientists, device engineers and clinicians can all use.

The honest ceiling for scalp prediction today (about 20 minutes of warning, AUC around 0.75, several false alarms per day) is not a reason to avoid the topic. It is the reason your project is valuable. Almost no one has reported surrogate-tested, alarm-level results on TUSZ, and no one has asked whether model-based inhibition rises before scalp seizures. If the answer is yes, you have a mechanistic early-warning feature that could later run on a sub-scalp monitor or a high-channel implant. If the answer is no, you have shown the field where the physical limit of the scalp lies. Either result helps the long-term goal of warning patients early enough to prevent seizures, not just record them.
